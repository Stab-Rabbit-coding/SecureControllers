# Manufacturer Activities — NIST IR 8259r1 and IR 8425

Sources: REF-NIST-8259R1 [1], REF-NIST-8425 [2].  Activity titles and the IR 8425 capability
names are **verbatim**.  Commentary is authored.

---

## The framing that matters most

IR 8259r1 §1.1 draws a distinction this whole skill rests on:

> This publication provides guidelines for **securable** IoT products rather than **secure**
> IoT products … manufacturers cannot create something that is secure in an absolute sense,
> but rather securable by customers in deployment.

And it defines the target:

> A **securable IoT product** has product cybersecurity capabilities provided over the
> expected life of the product by the manufacturer or other supporting entity that customers
> may need to mitigate common and expected cybersecurity risks related to the use of the IoT
> product and its connection to customers' systems.

Two things follow directly.

**First, "over the expected life" is a commitment you must size.** IR 8259r1 §1 notes IoT
products "are frequently expected to be in service for decades."  If you ship a controller
with a five-year signing key, a library you will stop tracking, and no end-of-support date,
you have not met the definition regardless of how good the crypto is.  Support lifetime is a
design parameter with cost, and it is set at ideation.

**Second, capabilities you do not expose are not capabilities.** A secure element on the BOM
that no firmware path uses does not make a product securable.  The customer must be able to
*use* the capability.

## The nine activities

IR 8259r1 §1.2 lists nine activities: six pre-market (§3.1–3.6) and three post-market
(§4.1–4.3).  They are presented sequentially and are mostly intended to be performed
sequentially, though some may be performed in parallel.

### Pre-market (Section 3)

| ID | Activity (verbatim) | §  | Phase in this skill |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Prioritize Cybersecurity and Maintain Cybersecurity Posture | 3.1 | Ideation |
| 1 | Identify Expected Customers and Define Expected Use Cases | 3.2 | Ideation |
| 2 | Research Customer Cybersecurity Needs and Goals | 3.3 | Ideation |
| 3 | Determine Appropriate Means to Support Customer Needs and Goals in the Context of the IoT Product | 3.4 | Ideation → Creation |
| 4 | Define IoT Product Cybersecurity Capabilities Based on Appropriate Means | 3.5 | Creation |
| 5 | Plan for Adequate Support of Customer Needs and Goals | 3.6 | Creation → Refinement |

### Post-market (Section 4)

| ID | Activity (verbatim) | §  | Phase in this skill |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | On-Going Support of Product Cybersecurity throughout the Lifecycle and through End-of-Life | 4.1 | Sustainment |
| 7 | Define Approaches for Communicating to Customers | 4.2 | Refinement → QA |
| 8 | Decide What to Communicate to Customers and How to Communicate It | 4.3 | QA → Sustainment |

**Activity 0 is new in r1** relative to the 2020 IR 8259 and is the one most often skipped.
It asks whether cybersecurity is prioritized *as an organizational posture* before any
specific product decision. In a one- or two-person hardware shop the honest artifact is
short: who owns security decisions, what the standing rules are, and what gets vetoed.

**Activity 1 is a gate, not paperwork.** You cannot do Activity 2 (research needs) without
having defined the customer.  "Anyone who buys a servo" produces a control set that is
simultaneously over-engineered and wrong.  A named expected customer — *a commercial UAS
integrator flying BVLOS under a waiver, in US airspace, with contract cargo* — produces a
tractable SL-T and a defensible N/A list.

## IR 8259r1 §4.3 — what you must communicate

IR 8259r1 §4.3.1–4.3.6 (Activity 8) enumerates the categories of information to communicate:

| §  | Category |
| --- | --- |
| 4.3.1 | Cybersecurity Risk-Related Assumptions |
| 4.3.2 | Support and Lifespan Expectations |
| 4.3.3 | Product Composition and Capabilities |
| 4.3.4 | Software Updates |
| 4.3.5 | Product Retirement Options |
| 4.3.6 | Technical and Non-Technical Cybersecurity Capabilities |

§4.3.1 is the sleeper.  **Your assumptions are a deliverable.** If your board's security
depends on the airframe being sealed, on the bus not leaving the vehicle, or on the
integrator provisioning keys correctly, those are assumptions that transfer risk to someone
else — and the transfer is only legitimate if you tell them.  An unstated assumption is an
unmitigated risk that both parties think the other party handled.

## IR 8425 — the consumer profile as a concrete checklist

IR 8425 (Sep. 2022) turns the IR 8259A/8259B baselines into testable criteria.  Even when
your controller is not a consumer product, this is the **most concrete, shortest** statement
of the baseline, and it is the right starting checklist.

### Six product capabilities (§2.2.1)

| Capability | Criteria (condensed from the normative text) |
| --- | --- |
| **Asset Identification** | (1) Product uniquely identifiable by the customer and other authorized entities. (2) Product uniquely identifies each component and maintains an up-to-date inventory of connected components |
| **Product Configuration** | (1) Authorized entities can change configuration. (2) Authorized entities can restore a secure default (uninitialized) configuration. (3) Product applies configuration settings to applicable components |
| **Data Protection** | (1) Each component protects data it stores via secure means. (2) Product can delete or render inaccessible stored data collected from or about the customer. (3) Protections are used for data sent between components or outside the product |
| **Interface Access Control** | (1) Each component controls access to and from **all** interfaces — and, *at a minimum*: (a) use and have access only to interfaces necessary for operation, all other channels removed or secured; (b) access-control measures on all necessary interfaces; (c) access and modification privileges limited. (2) *At a minimum*: (a) validate data shared among components against specified format and content; (b) prevent unauthorized transmissions or access to other components; (c) maintain access control during onboarding and on reconnection after outage |
| **Software Update** | (1) Each component can receive, **verify**, and apply verified updates. (2) Product implements measures to keep software up to date (automatic application, or consistent customer notification via the product) |
| **Cybersecurity State Awareness** | (1) Product securely captures and records state information usable to detect incidents affecting or affected by components and the data they store and transmit |

**Interface Access Control 1(a) is the single most useful sentence in the document for a
hardware designer:** *"Use and have access only to interfaces necessary for the IoT
product's operation. All other channels and access to channels are removed or secured."*
That is a direct, normative instruction to kill the debug header, the unused UART, the
bootloader pin, and the test pads — or to secure them.  Cite it when someone wants to leave
SWD live "for field debugging."

**Software Update 1 says "receive, verify, and apply verified software updates."** Verify is
in the normative text twice.  An update path without signature verification fails the
baseline outright — there is no risk-based argument that removes it.

### Four non-technical supporting capabilities (§2.2.2)

| Capability | What it demands |
| --- | --- |
| **Documentation** | Create/gather/store cybersecurity-relevant information throughout the lifecycle: assumptions (customers, use cases, physical use, network needs, data handled, expected I/O, assumed security requirements, applicable law, expected lifespan and cost of support); all components; **how baseline criteria are met, including which are not met and why**; design and support considerations (all HW/SW from all sources, platform, trustworthiness measures — secure boot, hardware root of trust, secure enclave — known risks and misuses, secure development and supply chain practices, certification results, installability); maintenance requirements; secure lifecycle policies; vulnerability management policies |
| **Information and Query Reception** | A point of contact that receives vulnerability information; ability to respond to queries |
| **Information Dissemination** | Broadcast to customers and public.  *At a minimum*: (a) updated terms of support; (b) end of term of support or functionality; (c) needed maintenance operations; (d) new vulnerabilities with mitigation actions; (e) breach discovery with mitigation actions |
| **Product Education and Awareness** | Educate customers on capabilities, EOL/reprovisioning/disposal, responsibilities, expectations and assumptions, back-up, and vulnerability management options |

The Documentation criterion explicitly requires recording **which baseline criteria are not
met and why**.  That legitimizes a gap — but only a *recorded, reasoned* gap.  This is the
authority behind the N/A form in `nist-213a-catalog.md`.

## Phase mapping used by this skill

| Skill phase | IR 8259r1 activities | Primary output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ideate** | 0, 1, 2, 3 | Customer/use case, threat model, SL-T, capability targets, hardware gates |
| **Create** | 3, 4, 5 | Control register with allocations to HW/FW/doc; BOM implications |
| **Refine** | 5, 7 | Gap closure, trade decisions recorded, assumptions written down |
| **QA** | 7, 8 | Verification evidence per control, assurance case, customer-facing docs |
| **Sustain** | 6, 8 | Vulnerability handling, update cadence, EOL notice |

The mapping is deliberately overlapping — IR 8259r1 §1.2 says the activities are "mostly
intended to be performed sequentially, but some activities and parts of activities may be
able to be performed in parallel."  Do not treat a phase boundary as a hard gate on every
activity; treat it as the point where that activity's output must exist.
